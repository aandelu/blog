Ironies and Caricatures

In Victor Hugo’s third novel, Notre-Dame de Paris, every character has, on the surface, easily categorized characters: 

Claude Frollo, the righteous archdeacon; Quasimodo, the monstrous hunchback; Phoebus, the gallant watch-captain; La Esmeralda, the exotic and enchanting gypsy. 

However, Hugo, a master of tragedy and storytelling, does not bore the reader with petty plots and personalities. The reader quickly finds each character filled with a sense of irony and a mockery of story-telling tropes. Let us commence in an un-orderly fashion, starting with the character I find most interesting and also most misunderstood: Archdeacon Claude Frollo.

When I say “misunderstood” I do not wish to say Frollo is actually better than you believe him to be or that he has more cause to feel the way he does than you think; it is, in fact, the opposite. Much of the discourse I have read online claims Frollo to be a nuanced, though despicable, character, and while I admit he certainly acts like a human being in this story, (as opposed to the irredeemable villain from Disney’s animated adaptation) I still believe he is a representation of a simply evil person. The irony here is the second most obvious of any character’s: an archdeacon is meant to be the epitome of virtue, a bastion of morality for the people to look up to and seek counsel from. In reality, Frollo has not only ceased to believe in God by the time we meet him, investing himself in semi-heretical alchemy instead, but he goes on to break a great deal of the commandments he ought to live by.

The only further layer to his character is that at one point he was the most devout, most studious young cleric with a suppressed sexuality, and that at a young age, he was orphaned and had to take care of his younger brother, and his most commendable act was certainly taking in baby Quasimodo and caring for him. But what significance does that hold when compared to the attempted-murderer-rapist we see in the present storyline of the novel? Frollo does not, to my memory, commit one virtuous act during the main events of the novel, and I would say we cannot call that man very nuanced.

The hunchback himself, Quasimodo, is certainly the most ironic. The hideous monster everyone reviles is capable of the most pure love and heroism. I do not have much more to say about him, except that his “marriage” truly melted my heart and displays Hugo’s dramatic genius perfectly. 

Our next pro(?)-tagonist is Phoebus. He is the farthest thing you can be from chivalrous: idle, gluttonous, and lustful. And yet, Esmeralda is completely infatuated with him, despite barely having had one conversation with him. Of course Phoebus uses this to try to sleep with her, but Esmeralda never gets past her obsession with her “hero”, which you can quite easily say is the ultimate cause of her demise. The secondary irony here is, of course, that she is persecuted as a traveling gypsy, when she is actually as French as the Seine.

There are a few other examples, such as the entire concept of the Court of Miracles and its hierarchy, which, although based on true history, was expertly satirized by Hugo. I was thoroughly entertained by just the names of the Kingdom of Argot, Empire of Galilee, and Duchy of Bohemia. Research them if you are unfamiliar, I certainly had to. My favorite, however, is most definitely La Sachette.     

She is a holy hermit, always praying and prostrating herself before God, living only on water and black bread. Her greatest enemies are the gypsies for taking away her daughter, and worst of all is Esmeralda, who constantly reminds her of her tragedy. In truth, we find out she was once a prostitute, her mutterings are constant curses against God and the universe for her pitiful life, and Esmeralda was actually her stolen daughter all along.

Victor Hugo was truly writing a masterpiece of dramatic irony here, and it is remarkable and delightful to read even nearly two hundred years later. But we cannot conclude this essay without mentioning the eponymous cathedral herself: Notre-Dame de Paris. She is a masterpiece of architecture, a grand edifice of divinity. She holds on her facade and in her halls every saint, martyr, and seraphim. 

But what is hiding inside of every church, no matter how great or small, except the sinners?
