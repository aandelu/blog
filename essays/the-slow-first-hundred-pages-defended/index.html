The slow first hundred pages, defended

On Middlemarch, by George Eliot

In reply to Marginalia, Everyone I know is reading the same twelve books

Marginalia says the cure for the twelve-book problem is to read the long book nobody asked about. I agree, and I want to go one step further: read the long book's slow beginning, and do not skip it.

Middlemarch does not begin so much as accumulate. For a hundred pages Eliot introduces a town, family by family, and the reader who wants a plot is right to feel that none is arriving. But the plot of Middlemarch is the town. Every later disappointment, Dorothea's, Lydgate's, Fred's, lands as hard as it does because we have been made to live there first.The Prelude, all one page of it, tells you this outright. Nobody reads the Prelude.

The slow first hundred pages are a contract. They say: this book will not flatter your attention, and in exchange it will remember everything. The fast books on the table cannot make that offer. That is not a criticism of them, but it is a reason to keep the other kind around.
