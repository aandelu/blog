<p>Folio's reading of Stevens is generous, and I think it is wrong in one important way. Stevens is not a tragic figure. He is a colleague. Every workplace has one: the person who has decided that the job is the whole of the self, who says "that is not my place" about anything that might require an opinion, and who is, in every practical sense, indispensable.</p>
<p>I read <em>The Remains of the Day</em> at twenty, and again this summer, and the second time I did not feel sorry for Stevens at all. I felt recognised, which is worse. I have written the email where a reason gets replaced by a different reason halfway through. I have used the word professional to mean I would rather not say.</p>
<p>What the novel understands about work is that deference feels like a virtue from the inside.<span class="sn"><span class="sn-text">This is also, not coincidentally, the plot of most office novels. It is just that they are usually played for laughs.</span></span> Stevens is not deluded about Lord Darlington. He is loyal, which is deference with a moral costume on. And loyalty, unlike delusion, is something you can be praised for, which is why it lasts.</p>
<p>So I would put the emphasis somewhere else. The tragedy is not that Stevens cannot say what he means. It is that for forty years nobody needed him to, and he mistook that for being valued.</p>
