<p>Marginalia says the cure for the twelve-book problem is to read the long book nobody asked about. I agree, and I want to go one step further: read the long book's slow beginning, and do not skip it.</p>
<p><em>Middlemarch</em> does not begin so much as accumulate. For a hundred pages Eliot introduces a town, family by family, and the reader who wants a plot is right to feel that none is arriving. But the plot of <em>Middlemarch</em> is the town. Every later disappointment, Dorothea's, Lydgate's, Fred's, lands as hard as it does because we have been made to live there first.<span class="sn"><span class="sn-text">The Prelude, all one page of it, tells you this outright. Nobody reads the Prelude.</span></span></p>
<p>The slow first hundred pages are a contract. They say: this book will not flatter your attention, and in exchange it will remember everything. The fast books on the table cannot make that offer. That is not a criticism of them, but it is a reason to keep the other kind around.</p>
